1. Using the planner, step by step

  1. Choose your dates. Type a first and last day, from 1 day up to 14, or use a quick pick such as "Next 7 days". Every date range keeps its own plan, so you can come back to an earlier week.
  2. Tell it about you (optional). Pick your gender, type your weight in kilograms and choose how active you are. These three things set how many calories a day the plan aims for. If you skip them, it uses general levels.
  3. Press "Pick my meals". For every day you get a breakfast, a lunch and a dinner. Each meal is one to three dishes, and the planner chooses how many servings of each (for example 1½ servings of a paratha) so the meal hits its target.
  4. Read a meal card. Each card shows the dishes, the serving size ("1 serving = 2 idlis"), and the meal's calories, protein, carbs and fat. Tap a dish name to open its recipe, or the green ▶ to watch its video. A small orange note appears only when a meal is a little off target.
  5. Change what you don't like. "Reroll" swaps one meal for a new one. Tick "Keep" on the meals you love, then press "Pick again", and only the unticked meals change.
  6. Use the shopping list. Open the "Shopping list" box at the bottom: every ingredient in your plan listed once, grouped by type, with tick boxes for what you already have. Amounts are in the recipe-by-recipe lists inside it.
  7. Get reminders. This site cannot send notifications itself. See section 7 for how reminders can reach you by email.
  8. Cooking for two? Choose "Me and my partner (one pot)" instead of "Just me" and see Planning for two.
  9. Print it. "Print plan" makes a clean paper copy without the buttons.

In plain words: your plan is saved in your own browser on your own device. Nothing is sent anywhere, so there is no account and nobody else can see it. It also means the plan will not appear on a different phone or computer.

2. How many calories a day?

A calorie (properly, a kilocalorie or "kcal") is a unit of energy. Your body burns energy all day just to stay alive (breathing, keeping warm, pumping blood) and more when you move. The planner needs a daily number to aim for, and this is where it gets it.

The starting numbers

India's national nutrition institute (ICMR-NIN) publishes how much energy a typical adult needs, in its 2020 guidelines (the figures here are as reported in published summaries). It describes a "reference man" who weighs 65 kg and a "reference woman" who weighs 55 kg, at three activity levels:

ActivityReference man (65 kg)Reference woman (55 kg)
Sedentary (desk job)2,110 kcal1,660 kcal
Moderately active2,710 kcal2,130 kcal
Heavy physical work3,470 kcal2,730 kcal*

*I could not confirm a published 2020 heavy-work figure for women. This one is estimated from the men's number, using the same ratio (about 0.79) that the other two rows show. The planner says so whenever you pick it.

Adjusting for your weight

A heavier person burns more energy. When you type your weight, the planner scales the number in proportion. A 70 kg man with a desk job is 70 ÷ 65 of 2,110, which is about 2,270 kcal a day. A 50 kg woman with a desk job is 50 ÷ 55 of 1,660, about 1,510 kcal.

In plain words: these are good averages, not a measurement of you. Age, height, muscle, health conditions and goals such as weight loss all change what you really need. The planner does not know any of those, so treat the target as a sensible starting point, and ask a doctor or dietitian for anything personal.

"Prefer not to say"

If you leave gender on "Prefer not to say", the planner uses the general (reference man) figures for a 65 kg adult.

3. Protein, carbs and fat

Food energy comes from three macronutrients. They are the big three; vitamins and minerals are separate.

NutrientWhat it doesEnergy per gramPlanner aims for
ProteinBuilds and repairs muscle and much else. In this planner it comes from dals, paneer, soya, tofu, sprouts and yogurt.4 kcal15–35% of calories, ideally about 20%
CarbohydrateThe body's main quick fuel: grains, millets, fruit, vegetables.4 kcal45–65%, ideally about 50%
FatFuel, and it carries some vitamins: oil, ghee, nuts, seeds.9 kcal20–35%, ideally about 30%

Because fat has more than twice the energy per gram, a spoon of oil adds a lot of calories quickly. That is why the planner looks at the share of calories from each nutrient, not just grams.

Where the percentages come from

The ranges follow the US and Canadian "acceptable macronutrient ranges", which are widely used worldwide. I raised the protein floor to 15% because protein matters in a vegetarian diet. I could not verify a set of percentage ranges published specifically for India, so these are borrowed.

How much protein is enough?

The Indian minimum allowance (ICMR-NIN 2020) is about 0.83 g of protein for every kilogram of body weight a day. At 65 kg that is about 54 g. The planner usually lands well above that because it aims for about 20% of calories.

A worked example

A 70 kg man with a desk job

  • Daily target: 2,110 × 70 ÷ 65 = about 2,270 kcal.
  • Protein, 15–35%: about 85 to 199 g a day (ideal about 114 g).
  • Carbs, 45–65%: about 255 to 369 g a day (ideal about 284 g).
  • Fat, 20–35%: about 50 to 88 g a day (ideal about 76 g).

4. How a meal is built

Splitting the day

The day's calories are split 30% breakfast, 35% lunch and 35% dinner. No snacks are planned. For the 70 kg man above, that is about 680 kcal for breakfast and 795 kcal each for lunch and dinner.

Finding a good combination

Think of it as a game of trying thousands of combinations. For each meal the planner picks a main dish, often adds one or two side dishes, and chooses portions in half-serving steps (½, 1, 1½ and so on). It throws away any combination whose calories are more than 10% away from the meal's target, or whose protein, carb or fat share falls outside the ranges above. From the combinations that pass, it picks one of the best, so the result is balanced but still varied.

  • The same main dish is not used twice in the same slot during a plan (no identical dinner two nights running).
  • If nothing passes, the planner loosens the rules a little. The card then shows an orange "Close" note that says what is off, so it never pretends.
  • Dressings and everything on the "Recipe shelf" are for browsing only. The planner never picks them. On the recipes page you can filter the shelf by style (Indian, Blue Zones, Mediterranean, Thai × Indian, Restaurant, Kids' lunch boxes or Dressings) and by main ingredient, and use both filters together.

The daily lunch drink

The Sattu Cucumber Yoghurt Drink is added to every lunch, one glass a day. The planner counts its calories and nutrients first, then balances the rest of the lunch around it. That is why the rest of lunch is a little lighter than dinner.

The morning routine

Every breakfast also starts with a tablespoon of extra virgin olive oil (first thing in the morning) and a soaked nut and seed platter: 2 dried figs, 6 almonds, 4 walnut halves and a tablespoon of pumpkin seeds. The day's seasonal fruit goes with it. The calories count towards breakfast, but the oil and nuts are fat-rich on purpose, so the protein, carb and fat balance is judged on the rest of the breakfast. The amounts are our assumption; change them if you eat more or less. Plans emailed before this was added keep their old breakfasts.

In plain words: the planner does the sums you would do on paper: add up the calories and the protein, carbs and fat of a few dishes, and nudge the portions until the total matches your target.

Planning for two (one pot)

If two people eat from the same pot, they should eat the same dishes but not necessarily the same amounts, because their calorie needs differ. Choose "Me and my partner (one pot)" in the "Planning for" box and fill in each person's name, gender, weight and activity level.

  • Same dishes, separate portions. Each meal card shows, for every dish, how many servings each person eats, for example "Sam 2½ · Alex 1½".
  • Both are checked. The planner tries many dish combinations and keeps only those where each person can reach their own calorie target and a healthy protein, carb and fat split. Each person's totals appear on the card and for the day.
  • The lunch drink is one glass each, so one batch of the sattu drink covers both of you.
  • One shopping list. It adds up the servings for both people, so you buy and cook once. The reminders show the combined portions too, such as "2 + 1½ x Paneer Paratha".
  • Sharing. "Copy link to share this plan" puts the whole plan inside a web link. Send it by message and the other person sees exactly the same plan on their own phone, with no account. It is a copy, not a live connection, so if you change the plan afterwards, send a new link.

In plain words: think of it as cooking one pot and serving two plates, with each plate sized for the person eating it.

If two people's needs are very far apart (for example a heavy-labour 100 kg man and a 40 kg woman at a desk), one pot cannot suit both exactly, and some meals will show an orange "Close" note that says what is off.

5. Where the recipe numbers come from

Most recipe videos do not list calories. So every recipe's nutrition is worked out from its ingredient list, in four steps:

  1. Turn each ingredient into grams. "1 cup of moong dal" becomes about 207 g, "1 tbsp of oil" about 14 g. Different foods weigh different amounts per cup.
  2. Look it up. The USDA FoodData Central database (the US government's food-composition table, the "SR Legacy 2018" edition) says how many calories, protein, carbs and fat are in 100 g of that food.
  3. Add everything up. That gives the whole pot.
  4. Divide by the number of servings. That gives the figure on each card.

What counts, and what does not

  • Counted: grains, flours, dals, dairy, oils, sugars, nuts and vegetables.
  • Counted as zero: salt, water, spices, fresh herbs, lemon and lime juice, vinegar, and anything with no amount given (such as "oil for roasting"). They add very few calories, but this does mean a recipe can read a little low.
  • Not available: when a key amount is missing and cannot be turned into grams without guessing (for example the dough in some parathas, or idli batter), the card says so and shows no numbers rather than inventing them.

What a "serving" is

If the creator says what the recipe makes ("makes 20 idlis" or "serves 4"), the card says From the source. If not, the site assumes a typical serving (for example 2 idlis, 1 plate of poha, a 200 g bowl of salad) and marks it Assumed. The full list is in a fold-out box on the recipes page.

In plain words: the numbers are careful estimates, not lab results. Real values change with the brand, how much oil or ghee you really use, and how big your portion is. As a rough guide, they could be off by 10% to 30%.

6. Where the recipes come from

  • Creator videos. Most recipes link to a YouTube video, and the ingredient amounts are copied from the creator's own description or recipe page. Each recipe has its own video, and none is repeated.
  • Home recipes. A family recipe with no video, such as the sattu drink, is labelled "Home recipe".
  • Recipe shelf. The vegetarian recipes on the shelf (everything the planner never picks) are folded away on the recipes page. Their amounts are for one serving, as each recipe is written, and I worked out the nutrition ingredient by ingredient. Where there is a video, it is a close match found by searching YouTube, not this exact recipe, so its amounts may differ.
  • Veggie Delights. 496 more vegetarian recipes come from an online collection called Veggie Delights. They sit together on the Recipe shelf. We restated the ingredient lists in our own style and kept the nutrition per serving from the original page. Every card links back to the original recipe page for the method. Where the collection has a video, it is the original video for the recipe. They are on the recipes page only and are never picked by the planner.
  • Main-ingredient tags. The recipes the planner never picks carry a tag for their main ingredient (Paneer, Lentil & Beans, Rice & Grains, Millet, Quinoa, Makhana, Pasta & Noodles, Vegetables, or Other), shown as buttons on the recipes page. For the Veggie Delights recipes the tag is the original page's own category. For the rest it was worked out automatically from the name and ingredients, so a few may be tagged oddly (tofu dishes sit under Paneer).
  • One fresh fruit every breakfast. The planner adds one fruit to each breakfast, a different one each day, picked at random from the fruits in season that month (US East Coast seasons), and balances the rest of the meal around it. The fruits are on the recipes page under "Fresh fruits", with USDA portion sizes.
  • Everything is vegetarian, with eggs. There is no meat or fish. The only egg dishes are two breakfast sides, boiled eggs and masala omelette, which the planner picks now and then.

7. Reminders

This site is a set of plain files with no server, so it cannot send notifications by itself. If you want nudges, an email assistant can run on a schedule, build the week's plan with the same planning code as this page, and email you the plan, a shopping day list and an evening reminder with the next day's menu, including anything to soak or sprout in advance.

The planning code and the emails are in the agent folder of the project, for anyone who wants to set up their own.

8. Meal prep: what the fridge and freezer really do

The "prep once" tips in the Saturday shopping email come from the food-science video The Science of Meal Prep by Masala Lab (Krish Ashok). These are the video's claims. We have not tested them ourselves.

  • Reheated food keeps its nutrition. Most of the vitamin loss happens during the first cooking. Only water-soluble vitamins (B and C) are lost to heat, and a side salad or some fruit makes up for them.
  • Cooled rice and roti change. Chilling cooked rice builds resistant starch, which the video says lowers the blood sugar spike after reheating. Chilled roti turns leathery, and reheating from frozen suffers less of this.
  • Dal thickens overnight. The proteins link up as the dal cools, so it turns thick. The nutrition is the same, and a splash of water loosens it.
  • Indian spices help leftovers. Turmeric, clove and cinnamon slow the stale taste that fats get from oxidising, and curries often taste richer the next day.
  • Freeze fast and flat. Small ice crystals damage food less, so spread food in thin layers or in flat bags rather than deep boxes.
  • Frozen vegetables are fine. They are blanched and frozen within hours of harvest, so they can match or beat vegetables that spent days in a shop.

9. What this cannot do

  • It is a general guide. It is not medical or dietary advice, and it does not know about diabetes, kidney problems, pregnancy, allergies or medicines.
  • It does not know your age or height, or whether you want to lose or gain weight.
  • The macronutrient ranges are borrowed from US and Canadian guidance because I could not verify India-specific ones.
  • Nutrition figures are estimates from ingredient lists. Some ingredients have no exact USDA match, and a few use a close stand-in (for example sev, papdi, and the tamarind-date chutney). Spices and herbs count as zero.
  • Recipe videos belong to their creators and can be removed at any time.

9. Word list

kcal (calorie)
A unit of food energy. The "calories" on a packet are kcal.
Macronutrient
Protein, carbohydrate or fat: the three nutrients that supply energy.
ICMR-NIN
India's Indian Council of Medical Research and the National Institute of Nutrition. They publish the recommended intakes for Indians.
RDA
Recommended Dietary Allowance: the amount of a nutrient that meets the needs of almost everyone in a group.
Reference man / woman
A typical adult used to write the guidelines: 65 kg and 55 kg in the Indian tables.
USDA FoodData Central
The US Department of Agriculture's public table of what is in thousands of foods.
Sedentary
Mostly sitting during the day, such as a desk job.
.ics file
A standard calendar file that Google Calendar, Apple Calendar and Outlook can all import.
